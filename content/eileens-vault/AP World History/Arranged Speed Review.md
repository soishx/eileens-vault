## U1
- Song Dynasty
	- Meritocracy: civil service exams were based on knowledge of Confucian texts.
	- **Neo-Confucianism**: A revival and reinterpretation of Confucian thought in Song-dynasty China that integrated elements of Buddhism and Daoism, emphasizing moral self-cultivation, social harmony, and metaphysical principles like _li_ (principle) and _qi_ (material force).
	- **Buddhism** remains the main belief system.
	- **Champa rice** boosted productivity and population growth
	- first culture to use **woodblock printing**
- In the context of what came before, the imperial bureaucracy represents a political continuity, since the bureaucratic system had been in place in earlier dynasties as well. However, its modification under the Song to allow more upward mobility represents a change.
- **Dar al-Islam**: A term meaning "House of Islam," referring to lands under Muslim rule where Islamic law prevails and Muslims can freely practice their religion.
	- contributions to **math, science, medicine**
- **Caliphate system**: A form of Islamic government led by a _caliph_, who is considered the political and religious successor to the Prophet Muhammad and holds authority over the Muslim _ummah_ (community).
- **The caliphate system** rose after the Abbasids fell to the Mongols. Turkish people establish sultanate or Islamic kingdoms
- South & SE Asia
	- **how Buddhism and Hinduism affected the region**
	- **Sufism**: A mystical branch of Islam focused on spiritual closeness to God through rituals, ascetic practices, poetry, and devotion, often emphasizing personal experience over formal doctrine.
- The Americas
	- The Incas: centralized their power through their road system and the mit'a labor system
		- **The Inca road system**: An extensive and sophisticated network of over 25,000 miles of roads and bridges across the Andes, built by the Inca Empire to facilitate military movement, trade, communication, and administrative control.
		- **The Inca's mit'a labor system**: A mandatory public service system in which Inca subjects provided labor to the state for projects like infrastructure, agriculture, or military service, in exchange for protection and basic goods.
	- The Aztecs: centralized their power through their Human Sacrifice system
		- **The Aztec human sacrifice system**: A central religious practice in the Aztec Empire where humans—often war captives—were sacrificed to appease gods, particularly the sun god Huitzilopochtli, to ensure cosmic order and agricultural fertility. 
		- **The Aztec chinampa system**: A form of Mesoamerican agriculture in which fertile plots of land were created on shallow lake beds using mud, reeds, and aquatic vegetation, allowing for highly efficient year-round farming.
- Africa & Europe
	- Focus: **state building**
	- Africa

		- the Swahili coast
	- Europe
		- **Feudalism in Europe** (a decentralized system of government): A hierarchical system dominant in medieval Europe where kings granted land to nobles in exchange for loyalty and military service, with peasants (serfs) working the land under obligations to their lords. 
		- **Serfs**: Peasants in feudal Europe who were legally bound to the land they worked on and owed labor and services to their lords, with limited personal freedom and no right to own land.
		- **Manors**: Self-sufficient agricultural estates in medieval Europe that served as the basic unit of feudal society, typically including the lord's residence, peasant villages, farmland, and communal resources.

## U3 Land-based Empires or Gunpowder Empires
- Manchus
	- invaded China from the north but not many changes
	- Continuities: neo-confucianism, civil service exam
	- Change: centralized power using the **Banner System**: A military and social organization method used by the Manchu during the Qing dynasty, dividing society into units called "banners" that combined military service with administrative control over the population.
- The Ottomans
	- took Constantinople in 1453 and renamed it Istanbul
	- **Ottoman Sunni Islam vs Safavids Shia Islam** (constant conflict)
	- **Sunni Islam**: The largest branch of Islam, which recognizes the first four caliphs as rightful successors to Prophet Muhammad and emphasizes the community's consensus in religious and political matters.
	- **Shia Islam**: A branch of Islam that holds Ali, the Prophet Muhammad’s cousin and son-in-law, and his descendants as the rightful leaders (imams) of the Muslim community, emphasizing hereditary succession and distinct religious practices.
	- **Devshirme**: An Ottoman practice of forcibly recruiting Christian boys from the Balkans, converting them to Islam, and training them for military or administrative service, particularly as elite Janissary soldiers. 
		- e.g. **Janissaries**: Elite infantry units in the Ottoman Empire composed primarily of Christian boys recruited through the _devshirme_ system, trained as loyal, professional soldiers serving the sultan.
	- **Tax farming**: A system where governments outsourced tax collection to private individuals or groups who paid a fixed sum upfront and then collected taxes from the populace, often leading to corruption and exploitation.
- The Mughals
	- **an Islamic group ruling over a Hindu majority in India**
	- **religiously tolerant** like the Ottomans during reign of Akbar the Great
	- **Akbar the Great**: The third Mughal emperor (r. 1556–1605) known for expanding the empire, promoting religious tolerance, and implementing administrative reforms that integrated diverse peoples.
	- **Taj Mahal**: A monumental white marble mausoleum built in the 17th century by Mughal emperor Shah Jahan in Agra, India, as a tomb for his wife Mumtaz Mahal, symbolizing Mughal architectural achievement and love.
- The Safavids
	- Shia Islam
- The Aztecs and Incas
- **Songhai**: A powerful West African empire (15th–16th centuries) centered on the city of Gao, known for its wealth from trans-Saharan trade, Islamic scholarship, and military expansion under rulers like Askia Muhammad.
- **Tokugawa Japan** established a military **shogunate** who eventually gets so fed up with the maritime Empires that he declares **sakoku**until we see them again in unit 6.
	- **Shogunate**: A military government in Japan led by a shogun, who held actual political power while the emperor remained a symbolic figure, dominating from the late 12th century until the 19th century.
	- **Sakoku**: Japan’s isolationist policy (1633–1853) that severely restricted foreign trade and contact to maintain social order and limit foreign influence during the Tokugawa shogunate.
- Belief Systems
	- **Protestant Reformation**: A 16th-century religious movement initiated by figures like Martin Luther that challenged Catholic Church authority, leading to the creation of Protestant churches and significant religious, political, and social changes in Europe.
	- **Martin Luther**: A German monk and theologian who sparked the Protestant Reformation by challenging Catholic doctrines in 1517 with his Ninety-Five Theses, advocating salvation by faith alone and the authority of Scripture.
	- **Sikhism**: A monotheistic religion founded in the 15th century in the Punjab region by Guru Nanak, blending elements of Hinduism and Islam while emphasizing equality, service, and devotion to one God.

## U4 Maritime Empires (also gunpowder empires)
- Technologies
	- **Portuguese and Spanish caravels**: Small, fast, and highly maneuverable sailing ships developed in the 15th century by the Portuguese and Spanish, instrumental in Atlantic exploration due to their ability to sail against the wind and navigate open seas.
	- **Dutch fluyts**: A type of sailing vessel developed in the 16th century by the Dutch, known for its large cargo capacity and efficiency, which made it ideal for trade and helped establish Dutch dominance in global commerce during the Age of Exploration.
	- **Islamic astrolabes**: Precision instruments developed and refined by Muslim scholars to determine time, prayer direction (qibla), and celestial positions, crucial for navigation and astronomy during the Islamic Golden Age.
	- **Chinese compasses**
	- **Lateen sails**: Triangular sails mounted at an angle on a mast, enabling ships to sail effectively against the wind and contributing to advancements in navigation during the Age of Exploration.
	- knowledge of the winds
- Empires
	1. the Portuguese: Prince Henry sought a route around Africa and also a means of **spreading Christianity**, and built a school to teach the navigators how to navigate. From there the **Portuguese made it to Brazil and eventually in 1498 to India**. In the process they took a **toll**, sold the **Spice Islands**, and started the **transatlantic slave trade**, transforming trade all over the place.
		1. **Spice Islands**: A group of islands in present-day Indonesia (notably the Maluku Islands) known for their valuable spices like cloves, nutmeg, and mace, which were highly sought after in global trade during the early modern period.
		2. **Transatlantic slave trade**: The forced transportation of millions of Africans across the Atlantic Ocean to the Americas between the 16th and 19th centuries, driven by European demand for labor on plantations and in mines.
	2. the Spanish
		1. **Viceroyalties**: Large administrative divisions in Spanish America governed by viceroys appointed by the Spanish crown, overseeing colonial affairs, justice, and taxation.
		2. **Encomienda system**: A Spanish colonial labor system granting settlers the right to extract labor and tribute from Indigenous peoples in exchange for their protection and Christianization.
		3. defeated the Incas and the Aztecs
		4. started extracting cash crops like **sugar and coffee** as well as mining **silver** in places like Potosi
		5. Catholicism
- **Trading Post Empire**: A form of imperial dominance based on controlling trade routes and key ports rather than large territories or populations, exemplified by the Portuguese in the Indian Ocean during the 16th century.
- **Joint-stock companies**: Businesses owned by shareholders who invested capital and shared profits and losses, enabling large-scale commercial ventures like overseas colonization during the early modern period.
	- **British East India Company**: A powerful joint-stock company chartered in 1600 that established British commercial and political dominance in India through trade, diplomacy, and military conquest.
	- **Dutch VOC** in Indonesia: controlled spice trade
- **The Columbian Exchange**: The widespread transfer of plants, animals, people, technologies, and diseases between the Americas and the Afro-Eurasian world following Columbus’s voyages, profoundly altering global diets, economies, and populations.

	- the banner system in China

- the Industrial Revolution
	- started in Great Britain
	- causes: right mix of access to resources, capital, and urban areas
	- After about 50 years, it spreads to the United States and Europe.
	- based on
		- the works of **Adam Smith**: An 18th-century Scottish economist and philosopher, known as the father of modern economics, who advocated free markets and the idea of the "invisible hand" guiding supply and demand in his work _The Wealth of Nations_.
		- **Capitalism**: An economic system based on private ownership of the means of production, free markets, and the pursuit of profit through voluntary exchange.
		- **Laissez-faire system (policies)**: An economic philosophy advocating minimal government intervention in markets, allowing free-market forces to regulate production, trade, and prices.
		- the restricts of mercantilism were gone: **Mercantilism**: An economic policy practiced in Europe from the 16th to 18th centuries emphasizing state control over trade and accumulation of wealth through a positive balance of exports over imports.
		- consequences of the rise of ideas: new transnational businesses 
			- economic:
				- **Unilever**: A multinational consumer goods company founded in the late 19th century, known for producing food, beverages, cleaning agents, and personal care products worldwide.
				- **HSBC**: A global banking and financial services organization founded in 1865, originally established to facilitate trade between Europe and Asia, now one of the world's largest banks.
			- social:
				- women push for equality
				- a new working class emerged who championed Karl Marx who envisioned a world where the worker rose up and seize the means of production 
	- **Factory system**: An industrial production method where workers and machines are centralized in a single location, enabling mass production and division of labor during the Industrial Revolution.
	- **James Watt**: Improved the steam engine by enhancing its efficiency, creating a practical and movable power source that powered machines, boats, and trains, fueling the Industrial Revolution.
	- **Steam engine**: A heat engine that converts steam pressure into mechanical work, revolutionizing transportation and manufacturing.
	- **Internal combustion engine**: An engine burning fossil fuels inside cylinders to produce power, fundamental for automobiles and modern machinery.
	- **Railroads**: Networks of tracks enabling rapid and large-scale land transportation of goods and people.
	- **Telegraph**: An early communication system transmitting coded electrical signals over wires, revolutionizing long-distance communication.

		- decline in production in Asia as European factories were a growing competitor

			- (less effective) **Self-Strengthening Movement in Qing China**: A late 19th-century reform effort aimed at modernizing China’s military and industrial capabilities by adopting Western technology while preserving Confucian values.
			- (less effective) **Egypt**, led by Muhammad Ali, capitalized on global demands for **cotton.** They nationalized their cotton industry and restructured their economy to benefit Egypt on a macro level.

## U6 Effects of Industrialization
- **Imperialism**: A policy or practice by which a country extends its power and influence through colonization, military force, or economic domination over other territories.
- the "excuses" the west and Japan gave for massive expanding:
	- racist policies
	- religious civilizing missions ideas like **Social Darwinism**: A theory applying Charles Darwin’s ideas of natural selection to human societies, used to justify social inequality, imperialism, and racism by claiming that stronger groups naturally dominate weaker ones.
- Real reasons for expanding:
	- access to **raw materials**
	- access to **markets**
- Big Empires: the Americans the British the Japanese the Russians and the French
- **Settler colonies**: Colonies where foreign settlers permanently immigrated and established political and social dominance over Indigenous populations, often displacing them.
	- e.g. British colony in Australia
- **Berlin Conference**: An 1884–1885 meeting in Africa where European powers formalized rules for the colonization and partition of Africa, intensifying the Scramble for Africa without African input.
- **Sepoy Mutiny in India**: A widespread 1857–1858 rebellion by Indian soldiers (sepoys) against British East India Company rule, triggered by cultural insensitivity and political grievances, leading to the end of Company rule and the establishment of direct **British Raj**: The period of direct British colonial rule over the Indian subcontinent from 1858 to 1947, following the end of the East India Company's control after the Sepoy Mutiny.
- **Century of Humiliation**: A period from the mid-19th to mid-20th century during which China suffered military defeats, unequal treaties, and foreign domination by Western powers and Japan.
	- **China Opium Wars**: Two mid-19th-century conflicts between China and Western powers, primarily Britain, caused by disputes over the opium trade, resulting in Chinese defeats and unequal treaties favoring foreign interests.
	- **Taiping Rebellions**: A massive mid-19th-century civil war in China led by Hong Xiuquan, who sought to overthrow the Qing dynasty and establish a Christian-inspired "Heavenly Kingdom," causing millions of deaths before being suppressed.
	- **Sino-Japanese Wars**: Two conflicts between China and Japan; the First Sino-Japanese War (1894–1895) over control of Korea resulted in Japanese victory and territorial gains, while the Second Sino-Japanese War (1937–1945) was a prolonged, brutal conflict that merged into World War II.
	- **Boxer Rebellion**: An anti-foreign, anti-Christian uprising (1899–1901) in China led by the "Boxers," a secret society aiming to expel foreign influence, which was suppressed by an international coalition.
- **Banana republics**: Politically unstable, economically dependent countries in Latin America or the Caribbean dominated by foreign corporations, especially those controlling banana exports, often with corrupt governments serving external business interests.
