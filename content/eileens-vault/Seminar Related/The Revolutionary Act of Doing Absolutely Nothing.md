### **Thesis: The Revolutionary Act of Doing Absolutely Nothing**

#### **Introduction: A Teenage Confession**

It starts with a moment of rebellion: I decide to sit in my room and do absolutely nothing. No homework, no scrolling, no productivity. Just staring at the ceiling. Five minutes in, I hear my mother’s voice echoing through the house: “Why aren’t you doing anything?” I have no answer, because honestly, I don’t know. Somewhere along the way, “doing nothing” became a crime, a sign of laziness, a waste of precious time. But what if doing nothing isn’t a failure of time management? What if, instead, it’s the ultimate act of rebellion?

In a world where every second must be optimized, every skill monetized, and every break justified, leisure has been stolen from us. The capitalist work ethic has brainwashed us into believing that if we are not constantly producing, we are worthless. But I propose something radical: **doing nothing is an art, a revolution, and, quite possibly, the key to happiness.**

---

### **The Capitalist Scam: Leisure Is a Lie (Source D - Weber & Source C - Muther)**

Max Weber, the grand philosopher of capitalism, would be horrified to see me lying on my bed contemplating the shapes of my ceiling popcorn. According to him, capitalism has turned work into a moral duty, convincing us that our worth is tied to productivity. This explains why I feel guilty whenever I choose to rest. My teenage ancestors (a.k.a. my parents) seem to believe in this idea wholeheartedly. They’ll proudly announce, **“When I was your age, I was working two jobs and still had time to do my homework by candlelight!”** (Really? Candlelight?)

Christopher Muther, in his article about Americans’ fear of vacation, confirms that this nonsense doesn’t stop in high school. Adults also feel guilty for taking a break, even when they _technically_ earn it. Americans work longer hours and take fewer vacations than most of Europe, proving that the **fear of rest is an American epidemic**. My own father hasn’t used a single vacation day in three years. When I asked him why, he just muttered something about “getting ahead.” Ahead of what? The grave?

---

### **Old Leisure vs. New Leisure: The Lost Art of Sitting Still (Source B - Eliot)**

George Eliot, in _Adam Bede_, writes about the mythical figure of “Old Leisure”—a guy who took long walks, smelled apricots, and enjoyed naps without checking his email (because, well, it was the 1800s). Meanwhile, “New Leisure” means **watching Netflix while answering emails and feeling bad for not learning a new language during our lunch break.** Leisure has become just another way to be productive.

Take my classmate Jason, for example. Jason is _always_ busy. He’s on the debate team, the soccer team, the robotics club, AND he runs a small Etsy business selling 3D-printed keychains. One day, I saw him sitting quietly at lunch. “Jason, what’s wrong?” I asked. He looked up, haunted. “I don’t have anything to do right now.” Ten minutes later, he was googling “best ways to maximize your lunch break.” **Jason has lost the ability to sit still.**

But what if we brought back “Old Leisure”? What if we sat under a tree, stared at clouds, and didn’t feel guilty about it? What if we let ourselves breathe?

---

### **A Solution: The “Do Nothing” Challenge (Source A - Cohen)**

Paula Marantz Cohen argues that **students should be taught about money, leisure, and death in school.** I agree—especially the leisure part. But instead of just _talking_ about leisure, let’s take it a step further: let’s require **one hour of mandatory doing nothing.** No phones. No assignments. Just an hour to exist. Teachers might resist: _“But what will students learn?”_ Well, perhaps they’ll learn that not every moment has to be optimized. That time spent simply _being_ is just as valuable as time spent _doing._

The first time I tried this, it was terrifying. My fingers twitched for my phone. My brain screamed, “DO SOMETHING.” But then, something magical happened. The world slowed down. I noticed the way the sun moved across my wall. I actually _listened_ to my own thoughts. And I felt… happy. Maybe even free.

---

### **Conclusion: The Revolution Starts Here**

We have been scammed into believing that every second of our lives must be spent in service to productivity. But leisure is not a luxury—it is a necessity. So, I dare you: try doing nothing. Embrace it. Relish it. Let the world spin without you for a while. Because in the end, the most radical thing you can do in a world obsessed with busyness is to **simply exist.**

And if anyone asks why you’re just sitting there, tell them you’re leading a revolution.

The Thin Line Between Protection and Invasion 
Nowadays, when technological progress is encroaching ever more upon the private sphere, the question of surveillance—of who is watching us, and why—has become extremely relevant. Not solved by the unfortunate incidence of 9/11 and the consequent extension of executive power, it is an issue that is more visibly in play than it has been in recent memory. For the most part, we as a society seem to have worked our way back toward a semblance of the privacy that we used to enjoy. But it isn't as if the conversation around this topic has come to an end. Indeed, two rather fascinating sources provoke the issue of surveillance and national/international security—the rather well-known Edward Snowden revelations about the National Security Agency (NSA) and its surveillance programs (Source A), and the somewhat less well-known, though just as fascinating, historical context of espionage and code-breaking during World War II that Bernard A. Frank provides in Downfall: The End of the Imperial Japanese Empire (Source B). They both have a significant bearing on the issue at hand. The extensive abilities of the NSA were revealed by Edward Snowden's leaks, especially through a program called XKeyscore. Analysts could use it to access an enormous amount of data about individuals and do so without prior proper authorization. The NSA defended what it did by saying it had stopped many terrorist plots, and it said that 300 terrorists had been caught as a result of using their surveillance tools. But it didn't provide any convincing evidence to show that those were actual arrests of real terrorists. And the 300 figure itself, even if we provisionally accept it, is pretty paltry for a program that, we were told, was working wonders. Conversely, Frank's retelling of American accomplishments in breaking Japanese diplomatic codes during World War II provides a distinct angle on the ethics of gathering information. In this case, the ability to intercept and decode enemy military communications was essential for the Allied forces' success. They gained a remarkable insight into the intentions and strategies of their foe. The famous "Magic Diplomatic Summary" allowed our highest policymakers to effectively understand the Japanese aims that were comparable in significance to what our strategies required at the halfway point of the war. Here, I believe anyhow, the real-life implications of invading someone's privacy appear far more justified when the information gained was not used for unsuccessful or secretive purposes but rather employed as a life-saving measure in a war that had by then taken countless lives on both sides. The duality of surveillance is illustrated well by both sources. Surveillance can serve as a means of protection, or it can go in the opposite direction and undermine the personal freedoms of those placed under its invisible yet watchful eye. The National Security Agency often justifies its programs in terms of national security. Yet when we step back and consider the situation vis-à-vis democracy and personal freedoms, the programs look much less admirable. On the other hand, the historical context of Frank's narrative wartime is espionage. It suggests a more allowable, more palatable justification for invasion of privacy that says, "This is okay, this is necessary, to win the war, to save lives, to prevent further atrocities, to ensure victory!" Victory was the endgame, and winning in this context required some unpalatable measures, but think about it: those measures are now part of our history, part of the precedents we set from one generation to the next. Comparing these two perspectives gives us the topography of a moral landscape where surveillance is continually under ethical challenge. From this vantage point, we can see that Snowden's tale is really a cautionary tale about the kind of abusesur of power that can occur when you set up an apparatus of mass surveillance in the name of security. But if you look at the kind of research that really undergirds the mass surveillance part of the NSA's work, you can see with historical and genealogical clarity that these are also surveillance ethics with quite a long pedigree. To wrap things up, we must see the ongoing back-and-forth over surveillance and privacy not as a contemporary question but as a reflection of our societal values. This is a complex issue, and the U.S. is working its way through it. It’s vitally important that the citizens of a democratic society remain engaged in any complex issue like this one, drawing on their part of the historical picture—as well as on the right to free speech, which almost always leads to some form of unpopular idea—to help shade in the work of these too-secret entities toward a picture that makes sense in a society that holds as paramount the right not to be spied upon. And if we can help this secret-to-society part of the entities work toward that picture, all the better.

My Name Is Anne Frank, But I Am NOT Dead 
There are two kinds of people who read what wasn’t meant for them: those who do it to protect, and those who do it to control. And then there’s my mom, standing in the kitchen with my journal. It’s open to page seven. casual, next to the syrup, like it belongs there. She’s neither a protector nor a tyrant. She’s just very bad at espionage. “I wasn’t snooping,” she says, as though that makes it better. “It was just there.” It was not just there. It was under a pile of old notebooks, wedged beneath my copy of “Highway to Hell,” which, yes, I partly pretend to have read for the aesthetic, but still. It was buried. That’s what you do with things that aren’t ready to be dug up. “I saw what you wrote about Dylan,” she continues. Her voice is soft. I am now supposed to open up. This is a confession scene in some terrible indie movie she makes me watch to build communication with her teenager. And that’s when I know I am doomed. Because this is the moment where a stronger guy would lean back in his chair and shrug. Maybe toss off something cool like, “Yeah, so what?” But I am not that guy. I am a seventeen-year-old with bad impulse control and a crumbling sense of invulnerability, and my mother has just cracked me wide open like a classified document on the wrong desk. I could try to throw her off. “Oh, that Dylan? Yeah, no, I just think Blonde on Blonde is a little overrated.” But it’s too late. She’s read the words. She knows. I grab the orange juice. I gulp it like it contains answers. “You should’ve left it alone,” I say finally, keeping my voice flat. And then, the worst possible thing happens. She looks sad. “I know,” she says. “I know, and I’m sorry. But—” And there it is, the but that undoes everything, including me. “Why didn’t you tell me?” I almost laugh, because it is so obvious, and yet she is asking. She thinks we are friends. “Why do you think?” I say, shaking my head. I don’t want her to answer. I just get up and leave. She calls out my name. I chew my lip and keep going. The thing about reading someone else’s words—their real words, the ones they don’t think anyone will ever see—is that it gives you power. Sometimes that power is justified, like when the U.S. cracked Japanese military codes in World War II (source A). That kind of intrusion won the war, saved lives, changed history. Or at least that’s what Frank says. It wasn’t about control—it was about knowing what the enemy was planning before they could act. Well, let me modify that. It was about control — about killing genocidal freaks for good reason. I suppose that’s the ultimate in control, once they are heading for you and screaming Banzai. Best you can do at that point. But other times, that power is an excuse looking for a reason. The NSA read emails, dug through browser histories, tracked metadata with XKeyscore (source B). They called it protection, but they never even gave us the names of the 300 supposed terrorists they caught. I mean, couldn’t we get one name, if this was real? One lousy terrorist’s name? How does this apply to me? It does not. I am not a national security risk. I am not an enemy combatant. I am not plotting anything bigger than a passable term paper. But my mother cracked my journal the way the NSA cracks emails—because she cared about my safety. Here’s the thing, though. I get it. Even if I don’t feel safe at all now. Because my mother is not the NSA. She is not a spy or cop or the cartoon villain I want to compare her to in my worst moments. She is, at the end of a very long day, a middle-aged woman who gets most of her news from CNN and thinks rap is probably too edgy but it’s okay if it won a prize from a committee. The key difference between her and the government is that she loves me and it is her job for 147 more days to protect me from the stupid side of myself. She is not the person listening in to private conversations just because she is in the mood today. She is Savannah from The Prince of Tides (source D), the little girl who heard The Diary of Anne Frank and immediately built a hiding place in the barn. Her invasion of her neighbor’s life was not about power. It was about fear for someone else and the desire to help the helpless. My mom read my journal because she wanted to see me more clearly. Because she was afraid I was slipping into some secret life she didn’t know how to handle. Well, I was, but it was my — MY — secret life. She wanted to know. So she read. Now she knows. It’s better in a way. I don’t have to hide my crushes anymore. (I still do but don’t HAVE to.) I could tell her everything’s fine. I could tell her nothing’s changed. I actually did tell her that but it was a lie. Something has changed. The way I sit at the table, the way I lock my bedroom door now, the way I think twice before I put anything real down on paper. Privacy, once broken, can’t be fully repaired. Otto Frank might have thought he was honoring Anne (source D) when he published the parts of her diary she had deliberately left out, but he still made a choice for her that she never got to undo. Well, she was dead. My mother, in a much smaller way, did the same thing to me. But I am still alive. So I’ll make a choice, too. I’ll keep my journal, but I’ll write differently now. I’ll leave out the parts that can be used against me, hedge my truths, shift the details just enough to keep myself protected. And I’ll make my own version of a Magic Diplomatic Summary about anything too important for anyone else to know. It will stay in my head.
