## **peer-reviewed academic sources (books and articles)** 

### 1. **Crouch, David. _The Birth of Nobility: Social Change in England and France, 900–1300_.**

- **Publisher**: Pearson Education, 2005.
    
- **Summary**: Crouch analyzes how knighthood evolved from a military role to a marker of hereditary nobility in medieval England. He emphasizes the exclusivity of knightly training (e.g., access to land, weapons, and mentors) as a tool for reinforcing aristocratic dominance. Useful for discussing **economic barriers** and the codification of noble identity.
    

---

### 2. **Keen, Maurice. _Chivalry_.**

- **Publisher**: Yale University Press, 1984.
    
- **Summary**: Keen explores how chivalric ideals, such as loyalty to lords and ceremonial rituals, were weaponized to maintain feudal hierarchies. Includes analysis of 12th-century training manuals and oaths of fealty. Key for arguing how **chivalry functioned as social control**.
    

---

### 3. **Prestwich, Michael. _Armies and Warfare in the Middle Ages: The English Experience_.**

- **Publisher**: Yale University Press, 1996.
    
- **Summary**: Prestwich details the astronomical costs of knightly equipment (e.g., warhorses, armor) and how these expenses restricted knighthood to the wealthy. Provides quantitative data (e.g., price comparisons to peasant incomes) to support claims about **economic exclusion**.
    

---

### 4. **Coss, Peter. “Knighthood, Heraldry, and Social Exclusion in Twelfth-Century England.” _Historical Research_, vol. 73, no. 181, 2000, pp. 1–16.**

- **Summary**: Coss links heraldry (coats of arms) to the consolidation of noble identity in the 12th century. Argues that heraldic symbols, displayed during training and tournaments, visually demarcated knights from lower classes. Supports analysis of **hereditary status and visual propaganda**.
    

---

### 5. **Green, Judith A. _The Aristocracy of Norman England_.**

- **Publisher**: Cambridge University Press, 1997.
    
- **Summary**: Green examines how the Norman Conquest reshaped English nobility, including the role of knighthood in centralizing aristocratic power. Discusses how training tied knights to their lords through **feudal obligations**, reinforcing vertical hierarchies.
    

---

### **How to Use These Sources**

- **Crouch/Prestwich**: Pair to show how economic barriers (costs of training/equipment) excluded non-nobles.
    
- **Keen/Coss**: Combine to argue that chivalric rituals and heraldry perpetuated social stratification.
    
- **Green**: Contextualize knighthood within post-Conquest Norman efforts to control the Anglo-Saxon elite.

## **primary source documents** 

### 1. **The _Pipe Rolls_ of Henry II (1154–1189)**

- **What it is**: Annual financial records of the English Crown, detailing royal income and expenses.
    
- **Relevance**: Lists costs of armor, horses, and knightly stipends, showing the economic barriers to knighthood (e.g., how only nobles could afford training).
    
- **Access**: Digitized in _The National Archives_ (UK) or published transcriptions.
    

---

### 2. **William of Malmesbury’s _Gesta Regum Anglorum_ (Deeds of the English Kings, c. 1125)**

- **What it is**: A chronicle describing English history and society, including knightly culture post-Norman Conquest.
    
- **Relevance**: Discusses the role of knights in maintaining aristocratic power and social order.
    
- **Access**: Translated editions available (e.g., Oxford Medieval Texts).
    

---

### 3. **_L’Ordene de Chevalerie_ (The Order of Chivalry, c. 1180s)**

- **What it is**: A French chivalric manual describing the rituals and virtues of knighthood, widely circulated in Norman England.
    
- **Relevance**: Emphasizes loyalty to feudal lords and the ceremonial aspects of knighthood that reinforced hierarchy.
    
- **Access**: Translated in _Medieval Conduct Literature_ (TEAMS Middle English Texts Series).
    

---

### 4. **Feudal Agreements from Ramsey Abbey (12th century)**

- **What it is**: Records of landholdings and knight-service obligations (e.g., “knight’s fees”) between lords and vassals.
    
- **Relevance**: Demonstrates how knighthood was tied to landownership, binding knights to aristocratic patrons.
    
- **Access**: Published in _English Historical Documents, 1042–1189_ (Volume II).
    

---

### 5. **The Assize of Arms (1181)**

- **What it is**: A law issued by Henry II requiring free men to own weapons according to their wealth.
    
- **Relevance**: Explicitly links military service to social class, excluding peasants from knightly status.
    
- **Access**: Translated in _Select Historical Documents of the Middle Ages_ (Ernest F. Henderson).
    

---

### **How to Use These Documents**

- **Economic barriers**: Use the _Pipe Rolls_ and _Assize of Arms_ to show financial exclusivity.
    
- **Feudal loyalty**: Pair _L’Ordene de Chevalerie_ with Ramsey Abbey records to analyze obligations.
    
- **Social stratification**: William of Malmesbury’s chronicle contextualizes knights’ role in hierarchy.

### https://www.houseofnames.com/blogs/Hundred-Rolls Accessed March 6, 2025

The [Hundred](https://www.houseofnames.com/blogs/DomesdayBook#terms) Rolls, [Hundredorum Rolls](https://www.houseofnames.com/blogs/Hundred-Rolls) or [Rotuli Hundredorum](https://www.houseofnames.com/blogs/Hundred-Rolls) was a census of [England](https://www.houseofnames.com/blogs/England) and [Wales](https://www.houseofnames.com/blogs/Wales) often referred to the year 1273, but were actually taken from 1255-1284. It is generally believed that five distinct collections in 1255, January 1274, October 1274, 1279, and 1284 make up what we now understand to be the [Hundred](https://www.houseofnames.com/blogs/DomesdayBook#terms) Rolls. Many historical references site the _[Hundred](https://www.houseofnames.com/blogs/DomesdayBook#terms) Rolls of 1273_ or the _Hundredorum Rolls_ of 1273 which is now found not to be any of the actual years in which the rolls were collected.

These rolls are often referred to as the second Domesday Book.

As with most rolls of the [feudal](https://www.houseofnames.com/blogs/feudal-system) era, they were an attempt to record population and land holdings for the purposes of taxation. In some cases, they specify the services due from tenants to lords which was typical under the feudal system.

Many of the Rolls have been lost, others have been damaged, those that have survived and are stored at the National Archives in Kew. In 1818, the Record Commission edited the then known corpus of [Hundred](https://www.houseofnames.com/blogs/DomesdayBook#terms) Rolls and attempt was made to publish the records. More recent discoveries are being collated by the University of Sheffield.

Knighthood and noble status came together at some time before 1190,
when there are strong indicators that the two had been linked, not least in
Andrew the chaplain’s implication that **knightly adoubement was the**
**lower boundary of nobility**. If there was one thing that was the catalyst in
narrowing knighthood to a noble elite it may have been the possession of
a landed estate. An indicator of how the possession of land and an independent income was affecting the status of the knight comes from the court
of King Richard I of England. In 1194 he issued regulations for the
conduct of tournaments in England. These included the amount that a
**member of each rank of society needed to pay to get a licence to join a particular tournament.** The tariff ran at twenty marks for an earl, ten marks
for a baron, four marks for a knight with land and two marks for a landless knight. Any knight seems to have had the right to turn up, but the
landed knight was treated differently. Although the regulations do not say
anything about how much land was acceptable as a qualification, we can
assume that a landed knight had to have an estate that allowed him – with
whatever other resources he had – sufficient income not to miss several
payments of four marks in a year. Landless knights were automatically
assumed to be men of less consequence than those with land: so a fissure
had opened up in the previously undifferentiated knightly continuum in
society.56 We see other symptoms of this. Knights who had substantial
landed estates were men in whom the royal administration was interested.
In England from the 1160s onwards, they were the men who made up
juries of inquisition in the counties. By 1220 we find a case in
Herefordshire where a man who had been knighted was removed from a
jury because ‘he is in a household and has no land’.57
The possession of land was not the only fissure opening up levels of
social status within the knightly group and breaking it up to correspond to
the evolution of hierarchical class in society. If **a landed estate and knighthood was hardening as the lowest boundary for nobility by 1190**, there
was another boundary closing at the top end of the group who called
themselves knights. As the tournament regulations of 1194 make clear,
**there was a group called ‘barons’ who were on a higher status level than**
**the landed knights**: they were Andrew the Chaplain’s nobiliores. Like
Wihenoc of Monmouth in 1081, such barons might be proud to be called
knights, but by 1194, when the title of ‘knight’ was being taken to be the
lowest grade of nobility, such men would not be satisfied to find themselves on an equal footing with owners of one manor, when they were
lords of baronies of dozens and even hundreds of manors. Yet ‘baron’ was
not a formal title at this time, and would not become so for over a century.
**Men of baronial status had to find a way of distancing themselves from**
**knights who were their inferiors, and they found it in the possession of**
**their banners.** Banners were ensigns of magnate power. Only a man followed by a retinue of knights would need to have one. Therefore a superior
grade of knighthood had evolved by the 1180s, when a distinction was
drawn between a knight, and a knight ‘carrying a banner’.
